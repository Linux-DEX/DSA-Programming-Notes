# Unsupervised Learning

Unsupervised methods describe a set of inputs. They do not optimize a labeled target. Use them to group rows, compress features, find items that co-occur, or flag points that do not belong. If you already know the quantity to predict, a supervised model is the direct tool. An unsupervised step can still sit in front of it.

## Clustering

Clustering assigns rows to groups so that rows in a group are more similar to each other than to rows outside it. “Similar” is a distance you choose. Change the distance and you change the groups.

Scale numeric features before Euclidean clustering. A column with a huge range becomes the whole distance.

### k-means

1. Pick `k`, the number of clusters.
2. Place `k` centers. **k-means++** spreads the initial centers so they do not all start on top of each other. Random initialization can need more restarts.
3. Assign each point to the nearest center.
4. Move each center to the mean of its points.
5. Repeat until assignments stop changing.

The objective is **inertia**: the sum of squared distances from points to their centers. It always falls as `k` rises, because a cluster of one point has distance zero. Inertia alone cannot pick `k`.

**Elbow method:** plot inertia against `k` and look for a bend where extra clusters stop helping much. The bend is often vague. Treat it as a shortlist, not a proof.

**Silhouette:** for each point, compare its distance to its own cluster with its distance to the next-best cluster. Values near 1 mean a point sits well inside its group. Values near 0 mean it is on a border. Negative means it is closer to another group. Average silhouette is a check that does not always agree with the elbow. Inspect both, then look at the actual points.

k-means assumptions that bite:

- Clusters are roughly spherical and similar in spread. Elongated or ring-shaped groups get sliced.
- Every point is forced into a cluster. Noise becomes a fake group or distorts a center.
- `k` is chosen by you. The data does not contain a true `k` unless the process that generated it had one.
- Means are sensitive to outliers. One extreme point drags a center.

k-means minimizes a non-convex objective. Different starts can finish in different places. Run it several times and keep the lowest inertia, which is what serious implementations do.

### Hierarchical clustering

Hierarchical methods build a tree of merges or splits.

**Agglomerative** clustering starts with each point as its own cluster and merges the closest pair until one cluster remains. A **dendrogram** records the merges and the distance at which each happened. You cut the tree at a height to get a flat partition. You do not have to choose `k` before seeing that tree, but you do choose where to cut.

**Linkage** decides what “closest clusters” means:

- **Single:** distance between the nearest pair of points. Can chain long, thin clusters.
- **Complete:** distance between the farthest pair. Prefers compact groups.
- **Average:** average of pairwise distances.
- **Ward:** merge the pair that increases total within-cluster variance the least. Close in spirit to k-means.

The tree is interpretable and deterministic for a given linkage. It is expensive on large `n` because it considers many pairs. It is a good fit when you want a taxonomy (species, product families, document outlines), not when you want a fast partition of millions of rows.

### DBSCAN and OPTICS

**DBSCAN** defines clusters as dense regions.

- `eps`: neighborhood radius.
- `min_samples`: how many points make a neighborhood dense.

A **core** point has at least `min_samples` neighbors within `eps`. A **border** point is within `eps` of a core point but is not dense itself. A **noise** point is neither. Clusters are connected components of core points, plus their border points.

DBSCAN finds irregular shapes and leaves outliers unlabeled. It does not need `k`. It struggles when clusters have very different densities, because one `eps` cannot describe both, and it still depends on scaling.

**OPTICS** orders points by how they sit in the density landscape so you can extract clusters at several density levels, instead of committing to one `eps`. Reach for it when a single density is the thing that made DBSCAN fail.

### Mean shift

Mean shift moves each point uphill on a density estimate until it reaches a mode. Points that arrive at the same mode share a cluster. The bandwidth of the density kernel is the hyperparameter. It does not ask for `k`. It is heavier computationally than k-means.

### Gaussian mixtures and EM

A **Gaussian mixture model** treats the data as drawn from several Gaussian blobs, each with a mean, a covariance, and a mixing weight. A point belongs to every blob with a **responsibility** (a soft assignment), not a single hard label.

Learning uses **expectation-maximization**:

1. **E-step:** given current blobs, compute how responsible each blob is for each point.
2. **M-step:** refit each blob’s mean, covariance, and weight from those responsibilities.
3. Repeat until the likelihood stalls.

Soft assignment is the right output when a customer genuinely sits between two segments. GMM can represent elliptical clusters that k-means would split. It can overfit if you allow a full covariance and too many components on too little data. Choosing the number of components with held-out likelihood, or a criterion that penalizes extra components, beats staring at inertia.

### Checking a clustering

There is no accuracy unless you kept labels aside for a sanity check. When you did, **adjusted rand index** or **normalized mutual information** compare a clustering to those labels without requiring the cluster ids to match the label ids.

Without labels, use silhouette, stability under resampling, and a human look at a few members of each cluster. A cluster that you cannot describe is a weak product requirement. If the clusters must predict a later outcome, measure that outcome directly. Pretty groups can be useless groups.

## Dimensionality reduction

Wide data is hard to plot, slow for k-NN, and easy for a linear model to overfit. Reduction replaces many columns with fewer coordinates. Decide whether you need a **transform you can apply to a new row** (PCA, NMF, ICA) or a **picture of this particular sample** (t-SNE).

### Principal component analysis

PCA finds orthogonal directions that capture as much variance as possible, in order.

1. Center each feature (subtract the training mean). Scale too if units differ, or the largest unit becomes the first component.
2. Compute the directions (eigenvectors of the covariance) ordered by variance explained (eigenvalues).
3. Project rows onto the first `m` directions.

The result is a linear remix. Components are uncorrelated. The first component is the single best line to summarize spread, which may or may not be the direction that predicts your label. PCA never looks at `y`. A direction of small variance can still be the predictive one. Do not drop it blindly if prediction is the goal. For compression and for multicollinearity, dropping small components is the point.

**Variance explained** tells you how much of the total variance the kept components hold. 95% is a common engineering target for reconstruction, not a law. Reconstruction error is `‖x − x_reconstructed‖`. If that error wipes out a rare but important column, the percentage was the wrong target.

PCA loadings (the weights of original features in a component) are how you try to name a component. If you cannot, you can still use it as an input to a model. Fit PCA on the training fold only.

### t-SNE

t-SNE places points in 2D or 3D so that local neighbors stay neighbors. It is a **visualization**. Perplexity sets the effective neighborhood size. Different runs and different perplexities move the picture. Distances between clusters in the plot, and cluster sizes, are not reliable facts. You cannot naturally transform a brand-new row into an existing t-SNE map. Do not feed t-SNE coordinates into a classifier and expect a clean production path.

### Non-negative matrix factorization

NMF writes a non-negative data matrix as a product of two non-negative matrices. Because parts cannot cancel by subtraction, the factors often look like **parts**: topics in word counts, spectral pieces in audio. The input must be non-negative. It is a good fit for counts and intensities, not for centered data with negatives (that is PCA’s setting).

### Independent component analysis

ICA looks for components that are statistically independent and non-Gaussian. The classic use is unmixing signals that were added together (several voices on several microphones). Independence is a stronger demand than PCA’s uncorrelated axes. Use ICA when the story is “separate mixed sources,” not when the story is “keep the variance.”

### Isomap

Isomap estimates **geodesic** distance: distance along the data’s surface, not the straight chord through empty space. A spiral or a bent sheet can be unrolled so points that are far along the curve stay far in the embedding. It depends on a neighborhood graph. A bad neighborhood size bridges a gap that should not be bridged and folds the surface wrong.

## Association rules

On transactions (a basket per row), rules have the form: if a subset `A` is present, `B` tends to be present too.

```text
Support(A)    = fraction of baskets that contain A
Confidence(A → B) = Support(A ∪ B) / Support(A)
Lift(A → B)   = Confidence(A → B) / Support(B)
```

- Support keeps you off rules that apply to almost nobody.
- Confidence is how often the consequence holds when the condition holds. A popular item has high confidence from any condition.
- Lift corrects for that popularity. Lift near 1 means the rule is no better than recommending `B` to everyone. Lift well above 1 is the interesting case.

**Apriori** uses the fact that a set can be frequent only if all of its subsets are frequent. It generates candidate sets level by level and drops candidates whose subsets already failed the support cutoff.

**FP-Growth** compresses the baskets into a prefix tree and mines frequent sets without the full candidate explosion. It is the one you use when Apriori spends its time generating candidates that die immediately.

**Eclat** searches by intersecting sets of transaction ids. It is another way to the same frequent sets, often strong when the data is dense in memory as id lists.

Rules are descriptive. “Bread → butter” does not say that promoting bread causes butter sales. Overlapping rules need a cutoff on support, confidence, and lift or you will read noise.

## Anomaly detection

An anomaly is a row that is rare under a pattern you learned from the bulk. The hard part is deciding that “rare” matches “we should act.”

| Approach | Idea |
| --- | --- |
| Statistical | A value far in the tails of one column, or a multivariate Gaussian |
| Distance / density | k-NN distance, DBSCAN noise points, local outlier factor (sparse compared with its neighbors) |
| Isolation forest | Random trees isolate odd points in few splits. Anomalies are easier to separate than points in the crowd |
| Reconstruction | PCA or an autoencoder rebuilds normal rows well and fails on odd ones. The error is the score |

Isolation forest and local outlier factor are the practical defaults on tables when you do not have a clean Gaussian story. Reconstruction methods fit sensors and images, where “normal” is a manifold.

If you have even a few confirmed anomalies, evaluate like a rare-class classifier: precision at the top of the ranked list, not accuracy. If you have none, you are checking stability and whether the flagged rows look actionable to a person who knows the domain. A detector that flags every weekend as an anomaly has learned the calendar, not an incident.

Fit the notion of normal on data that is mostly normal. A training set already full of unlabeled attacks teaches the detector that attacks are normal.

## Choosing an unsupervised method

- Spherical groups, `k` roughly known, large `n`: k-means.
- Unknown `k`, irregular shape, noise to set aside: DBSCAN or OPTICS.
- A taxonomy you want to cut at several resolutions: hierarchical clustering.
- Overlapping groups and elliptical spread: Gaussian mixture.
- Compress correlated numeric features for a later model: PCA, fit on train.
- Non-negative counts into parts or topics: NMF.
- Mixed signals to unmix: ICA.
- A 2D picture of neighbors: t-SNE, for the notebook only.
- Baskets and co-occurrence: FP-Growth, then filter on lift.
- Odd rows: isolation forest or reconstruction error, judged on a precision-at-k that someone will review.
