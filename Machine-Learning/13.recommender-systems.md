# Recommender Systems

A recommender is a ranker: given a person and a catalog, put the items they most plausibly want near the top, and keep what is shown short enough that someone will scroll it. The modeling is easier than the feedback loop you build it inside — the recommendations change what users do, so the training data is a record of the system’s own past choices.

## The task and its data

**Explicit feedback** is a recorded opinion: a rating, a thumbs up. Real but scarce and biased — people rate extremes more than middles.

**Implicit feedback** is behavior: clicks, watch time, purchase, skip. Dense but a noisy proxy. A long watch is engagement, not satisfaction; a click is decision, not truth. Most production systems train on implicit signals and treat explicit ones as a quality check.

| Concept | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| User / item | The two sides; an interaction is a pair with context |
| Interaction matrix | Users × items, mostly empty (sparse is normal) |
| Utility | Usually a score or probability, ranked and thresholded by the product surface |
| Top-k problem | You show few items; evaluation must be at k, not on everything |

## Content-based filtering

Model the **items**. Extract features (genre, tags, price, text embedding) and learn each user’s history as a profile, or train a model whose inputs are user features and item features.

- Works at once for a new item (recommend it the moment its features exist) and for users with any history at all.
- Fails at **serendipity**: it repeats what resembles the past. Its ceiling is user taste; it rarely stretches it.

## Collaborative filtering

Model the **interactions**. A user who liked what you liked will like similar things, no features needed.

### Neighborhood methods

Two symmetric views of the interaction matrix:

- **User-based:** find users similar to you, recommend what they engaged with.
- **Item-based:** find items similar to the ones you engaged with. More stable in practice because item–item similarities change slowly while user bases churn; “because you watched X” is item-based.

Similarity on sparse binary data likes cosine or Jaccard, not Pearson on gaps.

### Matrix factorization

Compress the interaction matrix into low-rank factors: user vector `p_u` and item vector `q_i`, so predicted affinity is `p_u · q_i`. Fit with SGD or alternating least squares (**ALS**) on observed interactions, with regularization — and with the tuning care from [6.model-evaluation.md](./6.model-evaluation.md), because an unfactorized matrix memorizes noise.

What factorization buys: the latent space doubles as an **embedding space**. Similar items end up near each other, so retrieval can run by nearest-neighbor search in the item vectors, and users and items live in the same space (which is why it is the ancestor of the two-tower architecture below).

Unobserved cells are not zeros. Treating every absent interaction as “disliked” poisons the fit; classical work masked them or used weighting tricks.

### Two-tower retrieval

The modern industrial shape: one network maps user (plus context) to an embedding, another maps item to an embedding, trained so affinity is their dot product. At serving time, the item tower is precomputed and the whole catalog is retrieved by approximate nearest-neighbor search, then a heavier model **ranks** the candidates. Retrieval and ranking split, because you can afford depth on 100 candidates but not on 10 million.

```text
catalog → item tower → item vectors → ANN index
user + context → user tower → user vector
        → nearest neighbors → candidate set
        → ranker (many features) → final top-k
```

## Cold start

| Situation | Working answers |
| --- | --- |
| New user | Recommend popular-but-diverse items, onboarding choices, context (device, location, time); collapse to population defaults until history accumulates |
| New item | Content features and item embeddings from side information (text, image); explore it deliberately on a controlled traffic share |
| New platform / sparse everything | Content-based, until interactions exist to learn from |

The exploration question is real and never closes: items that never got shown can never get liked. Deliberate exploration traffic (a small, measured share) is how the catalog finds out — this is exploration/exploitation again, in the retail shape it first appeared in inside [8.reinforcement-learning.md](./8.reinforcement-learning.md).

## Evaluation

Offline and metrics first, on a **time-based split** (train on the past, test on the later interactions — never random, or leakage from the future inflates everything):

```text
Precision@k  = relevant in top-k / k
Recall@k     = relevant in top-k / all relevant
NDCG@k       = like precision@k, but credit is discounted by rank and normalized
MRR          = 1 / (rank of the first relevant result)
```

- Precision@k and NDCG@k are the headline pair for implicit-feedback ranking.
- Compare against baselines: popularity (top sellers), and the current production ranker. A recommender that cannot beat popularity is not yet earning its complexity.
- Then the business quantities: CTR, watch-through, conversion, diversity of what users end up seeing. Offline gains often shrink or reverse online, and a threshold test on a small traffic share is the honest experiment.
- Popularity bias is a metric problem too: a model that recommends the already-popular scores well and feels dead. Report coverage and diversity next to accuracy, or accuracy will quietly optimize toward everything being pop hits.

## Online health: the feedback loop

Two structural behaviors to watch in any deployed recommender:

- **Popularity amplification.** Popular items get shown, get clicked, get trained on, get popular. Mitigations include exploration traffic, popularity debiasing in training, and diversity constraints in the final ranking.
- **Filter bubbles.** Narrowing the user’s world drives short-term engagement and starves the model of information about other interests. Diversity and serendipity metrics are the tripwires.

The logging from [10.deployment-and-mlops.md](./10.deployment-and-mlops.md) has a recommender-specific rule: log **what was shown**, not only what was clicked. Training on clicks alone teaches the model that its previous blind spots do not exist. Exposure logs are also what make corrected (propensity-weighted) training possible later.

## Choosing an approach

- Small catalog, rich item descriptions: content-based, or embedding similarity — cheap and explainable.
- Enough interactions to learn factors: matrix factorization or two-tower retrieval, then a ranker.
- Real product with real surface: the pipeline is retrieval (fast, coarse) → ranking (features, depth) → business logic (diversity, business rules) → presentation, with the grade of each stage tuned separately.
- Cold-start-heavy problem: content features are not optional, they are the skeleton.
