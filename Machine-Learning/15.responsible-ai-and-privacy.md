# Responsible AI and Privacy

A model trained on the world will reproduce the world’s patterns, including the ones we would rather not mechanize. Earlier notes treat “slices where the model fails” as an accuracy concern ([6.model-evaluation.md](./6.model-evaluation.md)). This file is about the same discipline with higher stakes: failures that concentrate on people (usually the people with least recourse), and data practices that outlive the project that justified them.

This is not separate from the technical work. Most of it is measurement, split design, and logging — the same machinery as everything else in these notes, pointed at questions of who gets harmed and who gets surveilled.

## Where harm enters

- **Historical bias.** The label encodes past decisions. A hiring model trained on ten years of approvals learns the approval pattern, including its exclusions. A model that copies a biased label reproduces the bias with an air of objectivity — the bias does not become fair by being printed by a program.
- **Proxy discrimination.** Remove the protected attribute and predictors stand in for it: postcode for income and race, purchase history for age. A model can discriminate while never seeing the category, and removing the column from `x` does nothing about the correlation structure in the rest of the table.
- **Feedback loops.** Predictive policing sends patrols where crime was reported, collects more reports there, trains the next model. Recommenders amplify what they already show ([13.recommender-systems.md](./13.recommender-systems.md)). The model is part of the system that generates its training data.
- **Distribution shift by group.** A model whose accuracy decays on one subgroup can degrade invisibly if reminders are sampled from the overall population — the group is small enough to hide in the average.

## Fairness: definitions, and why you must pick one

There is no single “fair” number. Definitions can conflict, and — for two groups, imperfect classifiers — the impossibility results say you cannot satisfy all of them at once. So write down which you chose and why, per use case: calibration-of-odds has definitions that cannot coexist when base rates differ across groups.

```text
Demographic parity   P(ŷ = 1 | group A) ≈ P(ŷ = 1 | group B)
                     the rate of positive decisions is similar across groups
Equality of odds     P(ŷ = 1 | y = 0, A) ≈ P(ŷ = 1 | y = 0, B)   (false-positive parity)
                     and the same for true positives across groups
Predictive parity    P(y = 1 | ŷ = 1, A) ≈ P(y = 1 | ŷ = 1, B)
                     a "positive" means the same thing for everyone
```

- **Demographic parity** suits access opportunities, where the *decision* is what matters; it is the wrong goal when true base rates genuinely differ.
- **Equality of odds** requires ground-truth labels; if the labels themselves are the biased artifact (arrests, not crimes), equalizing errors against a broken `y` is precision-engineered unfairness.
- **Calibration + equal odds** cannot both hold exactly for two groups with different base rates and imperfect classifiers. Every system picks a compromise; the unethical choice is to leave the choice implicit.

Available levers, in escalating invasiveness: resample/reweight training data, add/group-aware regularization, threshold per group (contested, and often the pragmatic protection), or intervene upstream in the *process* the model feeds.

## Auditing a model

1. Compute every headline metric **by subgroup**: precision, recall, calibration, threshold-at-operating-point. Averages hide concentrated failure ([6.model-evaluation.md](./6.model-evaluation.md), error slices by segment).
2. Probe the proxies: (a) vary group membership minimally and measure how sensitive the prediction is; (b) build the model without any protected attribute and check whether a related but legitimate feature takes over its predictive role — if it does, you have found a proxy channel, and the channel keeps working even though the column is gone.
3. Where possible, run a counterfactual test: minimally alter attributes that are changeable, and read the decision shift.
4. Localize the gap before proposing a fix: is it in the data (who was collected at all), the labels (who annotated, and what "good" meant to them), the model (does it have capacity/training weight for the subgroup's patterns), or the deployment (threshold, presentation)? The fix differs by stage, and a held-out labeled set that covers the smaller groups is often the first thing worth building.
5. Write the audit into the repo as a maintained artifact with scope and date, not a one-off chat message.

Report formats worth knowing by name: **model cards** (model, data, metrics incl. subgroup slices, known limits) and **datasheets** (provenance, consent, known gaps of a dataset). Cheap to produce; they force the conversation.

## Human oversight

Automate the action at the severity your evidence justifies ([6](./6.model-evaluation.md)). If the miss is irreversible or means judgment calls (credit, medical, hiring), the honest shape is: model produces a **ranked queue with per-item attributions and a confidence**, a trained human retains the decision, and the reviewer’s final answers become tomorrow’s labels — which are subject to their own audit, since raters disagree under time pressure and incentives.

Contested ground worth naming: **segment-specific thresholds** (defensible as equalizing protection, contested as unequal treatment); **using protected attributes in training at all** (sometimes needed to *audit* across groups, and the answer to that conflict is governance, not silence); and **disparate impact liability**, which in many jurisdictions is measured by *outcomes* of a neutral procedure, not intent.

## Privacy

Stakes recap: training data outlives the project; a “trained model” is, in a weak sense, a summary of how it was trained; logs from [10.deployment-and-mlops.md](./10.deployment-and-mlops.md) become new datasets; and model quality degrades gracefully under privacy protections (and often under unfairness mitigations — trade-offs again).

- **Data minimization.** The cheapest protection is not collecting. Every retention task in [10](./10.deployment-and-mlops.md) should justify the columns it logs.
- **Anonymization is weaker than it looks.** Dropping names is not enough — quasi-identifiers (DOB, postcode, gender) re-identify with startling efficiency in auxiliary data. `k`-anonymity (each record indistinguishable from `k−1` others on the quasi-identifiers) helps but leaves patterns visible.
- **Differential privacy.** The formal guarantee: a released statistic changes by at most a bounded amount with any one record added or removed, controlled by **ε** (smaller ε → more noise → less accuracy). Two deployment modes: noise on a released aggregate, and **differentially private training** (DP-SGD — gradient clipping plus noise, per example, so the *weights* carry a bounded per-person influence).
- **Federated learning.** Training keeps raw data on-device or on-premise; only model updates travel, aggregated in secure steps. On-device keyboards and some health setups run this way. Two footnotes from experience: the updates themselves can leak (combine with DP), and cross-device heterogeneity is a real modeling cost.
- **Retention & secrets logs** — apply data-deletion rights (GDPR Art. 17-style) to logs and training copies, not just the display database; and keep personal data out of prompts sent to hosted APIs unless the terms are known ([12.nlp-and-llms.md](./12.nlp-and-llms.md)).

## Regulation

- **GDPR (EU):** basis for processing, deletion rights, human intervention for significant decisions; **explicit** consent or authorization applies to sensitive categories. Automated decisions (incl. profiling) have special provisions.
- **EU AI Act:** risk-tiered by use case; banned uses (e.g., social scoring by public authorities); **high-risk** category (employment, credit, education, essential services) carries risk-management, data-governance, logging, human-oversight and transparency duties. **GPAI / foundation models** have transparency and systemic-risk provisions.
- **US:** sectoral (FCRA credit, HIPAA health) plus state consumer-privacy laws (CCPA/CPRA and successors).
- **Cross-cutting:** copyright and contractual terms on training data; accessibility; and the note in [12](./12.nlp-and-llms.md) that LLM behavior can shift under *your* feet (provider updates).

## Minimal viable checklist

1. Decision definition in writing, with severity, before modeling ([1.fundamentals.md](./1.fundamentals.md)).
2. Data documentation and consent basis recorded; personal fields justified column-by-column; log retention defined ([10](./10.deployment-and-mlops.md)).
3. Subgroup metric table + one audit method (slice analysis, proxy search) in the repo.
4. Fairness operating definition stated, with the definition you *sacrificed* named — the impossibility results mean there is one.
5. Attribution (e.g., SHAP) available at review time, not as a reconstructed post-hoc story.
6. Training pipeline replayable, telemetry with discriminatory features visible offline; a named owner for monitoring metrics.
7. Model card or equivalent shipped with the model.
